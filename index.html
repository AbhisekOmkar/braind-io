What you've actually built

Question 01

The biggest thing you've built with AI

A freight forwarder's pricing desk lives in email. One shipment is dozens of emails across mailboxes, threads and people. Subjects get rewritten, threads get reused, and the shipment number shows up late or never. Nobody has the whole story in one place.

Trace fixes that. It files every email about a shipment onto one record: the timeline, the people, the cargo, the ports and the status, with every fact pointing back to the email it came from. The email agent reads a short memory of that record before it acts. The eval system uses it as ground truth. It was the million-dollar feature for the company.

How it works: emails arrive through Nylas webhooks. Code groups them by reply chain, shipment ID and subject rules, and lays everything out on one evidence board. One bounded model call then judges the board. It cannot go searching, and its tool calls and tokens are capped in code. A strict type check saves the result.

Today: 25k runs a week at 98.4% success, 27.5k+ live shipment records, 8.4 seconds per update at the median, $0.11 per record.

What surprised me after launch: the first version let the model search on its own. It found 16.7% of messages at $2 to $4 per record. Making code do the finding gave complete recall at $0.11. Later, cost monitoring showed 91.9% of one week's spend was records being re-saved; one record was saved 56 times. 21% of customer requests were never traced at all, because nobody had asked for a completeness program. And the grader was wrong: a label audit corrected 159 of 490 ground-truth labels.

Trace in the product

The Traces launch video, 28 seconds, with the company marks taken out. One Outlook inbox with every shipment mixed together, one shipment's mails scattered through it, and the next one's. Then Traces: every mail filed into the shipment it belongs to, the whole thread in order, any mail readable in full right there, and a new mail that arrives and files itself.

Question 02

How do you know it's right?

(a)Before anything touches a customer, I ask what exactly must be true, and turn each answer into a check that passes or fails. In production that meant replaying real past emails through the system, without letting it peek at anything that came later, and grading the results automatically, with an AI grader only for the grey areas. Each customer had fixed pass marks, for example at least 80% right on when to reply and fewer than 5% wrong replies. Any miss blocked the change from shipping, and if the results were missing, the gate stayed shut. The answer key was real replies people had actually sent, not made-up tests.

(b)The worst failure: a memory update loop ran away. One shipment record was rebuilt 91 times in 24 hours, and the system was queuing 1,700 rebuilds an hour. Nothing looked wrong in any single run, so no alarm fired. We caught it through cost monitoring, when 92% of a week's spend turned out to be this churn. My mistake was building triggers with no counters or budgets around them. The permanent fix: updates now fire on counters with a delay, each record waits its turn in its own queue, and hard budgets live in code, outside the model. The rule I carry since: measure cost and volume the way you measure correctness, because the worst failures do not throw errors.

Question 03

Your machine

My setup runs on a machine that never sleeps. Several Claude Code sessions run around the clock, and my phone is a full remote control into them. Claude is connected to my meeting notes, Slack and our cloud accounts, and writes everything it learns into a shared notes repo that my teammates' sessions read too.

Two loops run on top. A devil's-advocate loop: when Claude finishes a piece of work, it calls in a second AI to argue with it, round after round, until the change survives. And a watch loop that looks at production, finds the cause of a defect, and opens a draft fix on its own. It never merges anything. People keep the judgment.

Yesterday it found five issues overnight and had a draft fix ready before I woke up, and one session prepared my client call from the meeting notes. It got one thing wrong: it flagged as made up something the customer had actually said, and one of its fixes patched the symptom, not the cause. I still reviewed every change, made the commercial call, and spoke to the client myself.

Question 04

Teach us something

Never ask customers what they want. Watch what they do. For AI products I go one step further: do not ask users what they think of the AI, log every edit they make to its output before they send it. Opinions are polite noise. Corrections are the roadmap.

Question 05

The thing that builds the thing

At Nurix I built a daily report for a bank's lending voice agent. One scheduled job pulls the day's calls, builds the 30-column report the bank needs, encrypts it and drops it in their cloud. It took about a week to build and then ran every day untouched, covering around 12,000 calls a day, replacing a manual export and doubling as the bank's compliance record.

Roughly 50 hours in, a few hundred hours a year out. The whole platform worked the same way: set up a metric once, and every call after that scores itself with no engineering.

Question 07

The AI Brain for law firms

One brain, four layers, and nothing ever sends itself.

1workflow in version one: ingest, ask this matter, deadline alerts
5pilot lawyers on day 30
0outputs that send themselves. Every one is a draft card a lawyer approves or edits

Deliberately out of version one

  • Case-law researchWestlaw exists, and public law is where hallucinations live
  • Auto-sendingtrust
  • A general chatbotunscoped questions are unverifiable
  • Fine-tuningretrieval plus citations wins at this data size

In words

I have built this once already, for freight forwarders instead of law firms. The shape is the same: the real knowledge lives in emails and attachments, the work arrives as a stream of matters, and people only trust what they can check. So I would build the law-firm brain the way I built the freight one.

Knowledge comes in from Outlook and the document system, every attachment is read, and every fact keeps a pointer to the page it came from. Each matter gets one living summary, written by code first, with the model judging only the grey areas. Each client gets its own separate database, so confidentiality is built in rather than a policy. The agents are narrow: one sorts what comes in, one searches the firm's own past matters, one drafts and asks only when a required detail is missing, and a plain-code deadline watcher that needs no AI at all. Nothing sends itself. Every output is a draft a lawyer approves or edits, and every edit is logged, because those edits are both the test set and the roadmap.

Version one is one workflow, not a platform: bring the documents in, ask questions about a matter with citations, and deadline alerts, for five pilot lawyers. Day 30 live, day 60 measuring how much lawyers edit, day 90 add the one routine document they draft most, behind a quality gate. Left out on purpose: case-law research (Westlaw exists, and public law is where AI makes things up), sending anything automatically (trust), a general chatbot (unscoped questions cannot be checked), and fine-tuning (search plus citations wins at this size).

In words, ten lines

  1. The offer: your marina's phone, whiteboard and paperwork, run by AI, so a call at 7pm in April still becomes a booked haul-out.
  2. Agent one, front desk: answers every call and email, quotes slip rates, runs the waiting list, and books lifts, launches and repairs straight into the yard calendar.
  3. Agent two, yard scheduler: plans each day around the lift and the weather, and when a storm moves in it reshuffles the week and texts every affected owner first.
  4. Agent three, paperwork and parts: collects insurance certificates before winter storage, orders parts the day a repair is approved, and flags when a slow part will break a promised launch date.
  5. Why they pay: one missed call in season is a lost $3,000 haul-out, and the office runs on one overwhelmed seasonal person, so $700 a month per yard is cheaper than any hire they can find.
  6. First five customers: go in the off-season when phones are quiet, run one local yard free through spring launch, then let the owner tell the five yards next door. Marina operators all know each other.
  7. After 50 customers the system knows how long every job really takes per boat type, real parts lead times by supplier, which forecasts actually cancel work, and slip prices by region. None of that can be copied.
  8. It also holds each boat's full service history, and that history follows the boat, so owners start asking their next yard to use it.
  9. What kills it: owners who insist on talking to Dave, whom they have known for 20 years, and yards that quietly keep the whiteboard.
  10. I would know in 90 days from three numbers: the share of calls handled without a human, the share of bookings the agent creates, and whether staff still update the whiteboard by hand. If the whiteboard survives spring launch, the product failed.

Question 10

Everyone improved, everything got worse

Every team hit its number by passing the cost downstream.

uptime from signing a client to delivering the project
2×quality complaints
2clients left. Nobody is lying, every dashboard is correct

In words

Every team hit its number by quietly passing the cost downstream. Sales closed faster by promising more. Scoping went faster by writing thinner documents. Build shipped more by cutting tickets smaller and putting off clean-up. Client Success answered fast but shallow. Every dashboard is correct, because the damage lives between the teams, in the handoffs and the queues, and the end-to-end time belongs to nobody.

Look first at the handoffs: take the last ten projects and split their time into being worked on versus waiting between teams. Most of the delay will be waiting, and most complaints will trace back to thin scoping.

The one change: one shared number above all four dashboards, signed to delivered with a quality gate, reviewed weekly by all four leads together. I built exactly this for AI pipelines, one board across the whole system where every issue gets a named owner and an alert, because green team dashboards mean nothing when the company number is falling.

At Nurix I led six to eight engineers on client integrations for a voice platform. I was on the hook for a bank's daily data delivery across 12,000 calls a day. I would delegate client calls earlier instead of holding them all myself.

Question 12

The hardest calls

(a)The hardest call: at Sidecar I scrapped my own design for the shipment memory system after weeks of work. It was finding only 17% of what it should, at $2 to $4 per record. Admitting in front of the team that my approach was wrong, and rebuilding it with plain code doing the work, was the hard part. The rebuild found everything, at 11 cents.

(b)The delivery failure: we found that 21% of customer requests were being silently dropped. The system decided to reply and then nothing went out, with no crash and no alert. My fault: I had built alerts for errors but nothing that noticed absence. The permanent fix is a daily check for missing replies, and my rule since then: alert on silence, not just on errors. It is now under 1.5% a day.

Question 13

Hire or fire

(a)My rule: if the output can be checked, build the system. If it needs judgment or a relationship, hire the person. Right call: I replaced a daily standup call and client report with one pipeline. A week to build, and it ran untouched for months. Wrong call: I tried to automate quality labelling with AI graders. An audit corrected 159 of 490 labels, and we brought the human experts back.

(b)Restructure first: move them off the client's critical path, cut the work into two- or three-day pieces that can be shown, and I own the client deadline with a buffer. My line: I can absorb missed deadlines, I cannot absorb surprises. The third time a slip arrives unannounced, or the client hears about a problem before I do, we part ways, and I say exactly why.

Question 14

Your first 30 days

Week one: ship a real change through their review myself, sit with each developer to see where the time actually goes, then pair with the most open one and finish their days-long task in hours, in front of them.

Week two: build the shared setup: context files for each repo, an AI reviewer on every change, and one automation for the biggest time sink. All opt-in.

Week three: every developer runs one of their own tasks through it with me. Their complaints tune it. Skeptics convert on their own work, not on demos.

Day 30: numbers, not claims. Cycle time and review turnaround before and after, plus one two-week feature shipped in three days. If the numbers are not there, I say so. Five times is what I believe, not what I promise. Then reusable skills for each repo and task, aiming for ten times.
