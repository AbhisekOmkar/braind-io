Part 1

Bizquip

The cross-sell engine and the Sales OS. Nine things slowed the build or hurt after launch. One answer each.

Bizquip 01

Getting into the client's network took weeks

What happened

Reaching the client's ERP from outside needed VPN routing, and reading it directly was slow.

What I would do

  • Put a small box insidea tiny program sits next to the ERP, inside the client's own network, and calls out to us. Nothing is opened from the outside, so there is no VPN project.
  • Copy the data out nightlythe numbers land in our own database once a night. Reports run on the copy, so the ERP is never slowed down.
  • A green or red lightthe box reports every hour. If it goes quiet, the dashboard turns red the same day.

Why this works

One small box per system, nothing opened to the outside. The engine never talks to the ERP itself, only to our own small service in front of it, so the ERP and the firewall are never a project.

Bizquip 02

Nobody knew which ERP data to use

What happened

It was unclear which part of Intact held order history and won deals, so the build guessed.

What I would do

  • Write it down firstfor each report the client wants, write which table and field it comes from, with one real order as the example.
  • Check twenty real orderspull twenty orders the client knows by heart and compare field by field. Fix until all twenty match.
  • Keep the list in the codewhen the ERP changes, this check fails first and tells us.

Why this works

Twenty real records settle the argument in a day. After that the check runs on every record and stores what matched and what did not, so a change in the ERP shows up as a failed check, not a wrong report.

Bizquip 03

Email access had to come from the client

What happened

The build waited on the client's IT team for Outlook access, and nobody could see the wait in the plan.

What I would do

While reading this I recognised it at once. In my last role we hit the same wall: every customer ran on Outlook, and every project stalled on permissions.

First we used Nylas to reach Microsoft. It worked, but every employee had to click their own consent link, and each time we needed one more permission, every one of them had to click again. Onboarding a company meant chasing thirty people.

So I built our own email reader from scratch, on Microsoft's own interface. Now the client's IT admin approves once, with one link, and ticks the mailboxes we may read. From then on we control the access on our side: mail, calendar, out-of-office, all of it.

If we add or remove what we read, nobody in the client's company has to consent again. The admin's one approval covers it. When a mailbox joins or leaves, the admin ticks or unticks it. Five minutes, once.

We never ask for permission to send. The engine writes a draft into the rep's Outlook, and the rep reads, edits and sends. The consent screen stays short, and the rep stays in charge.

For Bizquip I would do exactly this: the access becomes one link with a name and a date on the plan, and the build runs on sample data until the admin clicks it. Today that reader runs seventy-plus mailboxes across nine customers.

Bizquip 04

Recent data was still moving in

What happened

Recent months looked low because the data behind them had not fully arrived.

What I would do

  • Every chart says its dateeach number shows "complete up to" a date, and stops there.
  • Count both sidesrows per month in the ERP against rows per month with us. A short month shows red before anyone reads a chart.
  • Show the trend tooan overall average never stands alone. The monthly line goes next to it, so a fall cannot hide.

Why this works

A count on both sides is the only check that catches missing data before a chart does. One such count once showed that a fifth of requests were never arriving at all.

Bizquip 05

Company names did not match

What happened

One customer appeared under several spellings, so the matching made duplicates and missed links.

What I would do

  • Match on numbers, not namescustomer number, email domain, company registration number. Names come last.
  • Tidy the namesdrop Ltd, Limited, PLC and punctuation. Keep a list of known aliases so each fix is made once.
  • Never guesswhen two names might be the same company, a person decides, with both records side by side.

Why this works

Numbers do not have spellings. Matching on them first removes most duplicates, and refusing to guess on the rest is cheaper than one wrong merge.

Bizquip 06

Signals came in repeated and noisy

What happened

Website visits, funding news and "same building" arrived many times over, so the list filled with repeats.

What I would do

  • Drop repeatseach signal gets a stamp: company, type, source, day. A repeat is dropped, not shown twice.
  • One a dayone signal per company per day, and a distance limit on "nearby" signals.
  • Test before switching onlabel a hundred signals by hand, count how many are right per source, and give every source an off switch.

Why this works

A stamp per signal, keyed on the one ID that never changes, drops repeats before anyone sees them. One alert per cause, not one per event, keeps the list short enough to be read.

Bizquip 07

Margin questions had no cost data behind them

What happened

People asked the assistant about margins, but the cost data had not arrived.

What I would do

  • Say "not available"the assistant says which data is missing and who owns it, instead of guessing.
  • Answer in stagesrevenue answers now, margin answers on the day cost data lands, with that date shown.

Why this works

Two steps: first list what is missing, then answer only with the rest. A missing number is asked for, never filled in, so nobody trusts a margin that was never computed.

Bizquip 08

418 found, 49 used

What happened

The engine found 418 opportunities. 49 were acted on. 354 sat untouched.

What I would do

  • Measure what got used"acted on" sits on the dashboard next to "found", from day one.
  • A name and a date on eachevery opportunity has a rep and a due date. Untouched ones go to the sales lead every week.
  • Take it to the repa ready draft in their Outlook and a nudge in Teams. Nobody has to open a dashboard.
  • Write down every actionsent, edited, ignored. That tells us what to show next week.

Why this works

Nothing sends itself. Every draft is checked back every two hours and marked sent, edited, deleted or ignored, and a draft untouched for seven days counts as ignored. The daily numbers go to Slack, so the 354 untouched are seen every week, not at the review.

Bizquip 09

Broken for ten days

What happened

One bad character broke the email step for ten days while the project still counted as delivered.

What I would do

  • Alarm on silencecount outputs every day. Zero pages someone. Errors are loud; silence is what hurts.
  • Clean text at the doorstrip bad characters before anything is saved or sent. Cheap, and it never happens again.
  • Done means the client sees ita project is delivered when the client sees results in their own system, not when our dashboard is green.

Why this works

Stray characters are a one-line fix once, at the door. The bigger point is the alarm: a detector for "someone asked, we decided to answer, nothing went out" catches what no error log shows, because a broken step throws no error, it just goes quiet.

Part 2

Tecknuovo

The delivery second brain. The classifier was the easy part.

Tecknuovo

The classifier was easy. Everything around it was hard.

What happened

Hosting, permissions, a security review, a GDPR question, IP wording, a mountain of duplicate signals, and a dashboard nobody wanted to open.

What I would do

  • Run it on their machineone VM inside Tecknuovo's own Azure. We log in through their VPN. Hosting, permissions and the security review shrink to one question: who may log into that VM.
  • Data never leavespersonal data stays in the UK, in their tenant. Engineers in India work through the VPN, see the system and its logs, and export nothing. That answers the GDPR question and most of the due diligence.
  • IP by namewhat lives on their VM is theirs: data, rules, setup. The engine and connectors we bring are ours. Two lists, no adjectives.
  • A queue and a de-dup stepevery signal goes into a queue. A stamp (account, type, source, day) drops repeats before anyone sees them. One line per cause, and each source has an off switch.
  • Slack, not a dashboarda daily digest and one thread per signal in Slack, with buttons to act. The dashboard stays as the record.

Why this works

A whole stack can run as a set of services on one machine with one command, secrets pulled from a vault, nothing exposed to the outside. Inside their tenant, most of the security questions answer themselves. The queue with a de-dup stamp keeps the signal list short, and Slack puts it where people already look.

Part 3

Johnston Shopfitters

The AI Command Centre. Sequencing, suppliers and a cloud that did not exist yet.

Johnston Shopfitters

Five platforms landing at once, and a cloud that did not exist yet

What happened

The plan was an AI Command Centre over Sage 200, Sage CRM, SharePoint and more. The client was installing five platforms at the same time, had no Azure, was quoted €11.5k for a landing zone, and the network path to Sage depended on another supplier.

What I would do

  • Scope moved, and build started before the paperwork was visibleone scope page with three lists: in this build, later, and adjacent asks. A new idea enters as a ticket with a price. Build starts when the checklist is green, and everyone can see the checklist.a gate in code cannot be skipped: not a duplicate, a ticket, an owner, a link. Scope stops drifting when every change has a price on it.
  • €11.5k and 11 days for a landing zone, before a 12-16 week buildsplit it. A small development foundation now, production hardening later, with the deferred cost written down so nobody is surprised. Pick the region on day one.a stack that runs on a laptop with one command, the same services as production, needs no landing zone to start. Hardening is a later, separate step with its own price.
  • Legal and data protection gated access even when the tech was simplethe same control pack as Tecknuovo, answered once: guest access, audit logging, screening, sub-processors. It goes to the client with the proposal, not after.an append-only audit log and least-privilege access are cheap to build once. The pack is a document around them, and it gets shorter every time it is reused.
  • Sage endpoints and the network path depended on another supplierthe small box again, beside Sage, calling out. And a written list of which data we need from Sage, proved against twenty real records, so the supplier has a spec, not a conversation.the engine never calls Sage. It calls our own small service in front of it, which is the only thing that changes when Sage or the supplier does.
  • Design the AI layer while five platforms are still being configureddo not wait. Write the data contract first (customers, quotes, jobs, invoices, status) and connect each platform to it as it lands. The AI layer reads the contract, never a platform directly, so calcified processes cannot lock it in.one record per customer, quote or job, whatever the source, with every fact pointing back to where it came from, means a platform can be swapped without touching the AI layer.

The short version

The job here is sequencing and supplier management, not model choice. Small foundation now, contract first, one box per system, control pack with the proposal.

Part 4

CWSI

Sales enrichment and outreach. Live everywhere, wrong in the gaps.

CWSI

Everything was live, and the numbers were still wrong

What happened

Lead scoring landed in Salesforce, sequences ran in Outreach, a results dashboard was built. Then a workflow kept crashing, a formatting failure was invisible, meetings showed as zero, bounces hit 20.4%, and credentials kept expiring.

What I would do

  • The KPI workflow crashed and n8n switched it offown the workflow: retries with back-off, a dead-letter list, a restart button, and an alarm when a scheduled job did not run. A job that did not run is the loud case, not the quiet one.three retries with back-off, a health and a restart endpoint for every job, and four states for every outgoing message: pending, delivered, suppressed or dead. A switched-off workflow becomes a red tile within the hour.
  • Sequences were created through the API by someone who could not open the platformnobody ships to a platform they cannot see. Every automated write is read back and compared to what we meant to write. If it does not match, it is a red tile, not a client email.read-after-write is the only check that catches a formatting failure the reporting data cannot see. A state for "claimed delivery, not confirmed" is what stops a broken sequence reaching a client.
  • Zero meetings, because reps did not click the buttonnever count on a button. Count from evidence: calendar events, reply classification, CRM stage changes. The button becomes a check, not the source.the mailbox and the calendar do not forget to click. Reading them back every two hours gives the real meeting count, and the button becomes a cross-check.
  • 20.4% bounces, hundreds of opens, zero replies, and no way to tell whyclassify every inbound message: bounce, auto-reply, out of office, real reply. Then "copy problem", "audience problem" and "reply capture broken" become three separate numbers per source.labelling bounces and auto-replies before anything else runs turns one confusing number into three clear ones. Real out-of-office state is read from the mailbox settings, not guessed from text.
  • Cumulative averages made the account look healthier than the trendthe monthly line sits next to every average, always. And two windows, because yesterday is never complete.a trend line next to an average cannot be argued with. Two windows stop yesterday's incomplete day from dragging the number.
  • OAuth scopes kept needing client-side reconnectionwatch the credentials, not the failures. Warn 30 days before a secret expires. When a connection dies, the owner gets a reconnect link automatically, and a temporary error is never treated as a dead one.a check every 12 hours that warns 30 days ahead turns a surprise outage into a calendar item. A reconnect link sent automatically to the owner removes the wait on us, and separating temporary errors from dead ones stops false alarms.

The short version

CWSI failed in the gaps between APIs, people and monitoring. The fix is read-after-write, classify every inbound, count from evidence, and watch credentials before they die.

Part 5

Daisy

The failure case. Both scoreboards disagreed.

Daisy

The chatbot worked. The system was not under control.

What happened

Daisy captured leads and booked calls, but wrote to a Make.com layer owned by an outside contractor. Braind saw green in its own dashboard while the client saw wrong deals in Pipedrive. Four booking incidents were treated as separate bugs, the contractor left, the client did more and more manual work, and then asked to pause.

What I would do

  • Four incidents in eight weeks, each treated as a one-offone incident board for every project. Every bug on one list, grouped by cause and ranked. Two incidents on the same integration in a month trigger an audit of the whole layer.four incidents on one list, grouped by cause, look like one problem. Four incidents in four chats look like four bugs. The board is what makes the pattern visible.
  • Two scoreboards: our dashboard green, their CRM redthe client's system is the only scoreboard. We read Pipedrive back and compare what landed with what we sent, every day. Our dashboard shows their numbers.the state is what the other system says, never what we intended. Reading Pipedrive back every day would have shown red on our side the same week the client saw it.
  • Routing depended on exact wordinga written schema with versions. Keys, labels and tags are named once, and a test fails the moment one changes. The seven-question flow would have broken a test, not a client.a schema with a test fails loudly, at start-up, in front of a developer. Exact wording fails quietly, in production, in front of the client.
  • The critical layer belonged to a contractor who leftwe own the integration layer, or we have the logs and a second named owner. A monthly continuity check: who can fix this if the owner is gone?a layer we cannot see is a layer we cannot fix. Owning it, or at least its logs and a second owner, is the whole difference between a bug and a lost client.
  • The client's workload grew, and nobody counted itclient effort is a health number: tickets raised, screenshots sent, tests run, records fixed by hand. When it climbs, the project turns amber, whatever our dashboard says.client effort is the earliest churn signal there is, and it is free to count. A rising line of screenshots and manual fixes says more than any green dashboard.
  • Silence was read as satisfactionsilence is a signal. A normally detailed client going quiet sets off a check-in, and "nothing went out" is detected the same way as an error.silence looks exactly like success in every log. Only a check that expects output, and counts its absence, can tell the two apart.

The short version

The missing capability was not a model. It was one board, one scoreboard, a schema, an owner, and treating silence as a signal.

Part 6

The twelve patterns

Your list, with one line each on what I would put in place.

One line each
  • 1. Integration ownership without visibilitywe own the layer, or we have its logs, a health signal and a second named owner. Otherwise it is not live.
  • 2. Dependencies discovered too lateone dependency list per project with owner, date and automatic chasing. Access arrives before developers, or the plan shows why not.
  • 3. No shared definition of donesix states, six words: built, tested, deployed, accepted, adopted, paying off. "Delivered" is retired.
  • 4. Technical success without adoptionacted-on rate is the first number on every dashboard. Output goes where people already work. Untouched work has an owner.
  • 5. Weak or incomparable baselinesmeasure the client's original problem, not our scope. Same reporting window everywhere. Trend next to every average. "100% accuracy" needs an answer key or it is not a number.
  • 6. Monitoring that reports output, not truthread every write back from the other system. Alarm on silence, not only on errors. Three sources of truth on one board.
  • 7. Data quality becomes product qualitystable keys before names, tidy names, a human queue for the rest, and a duplicate rate on the dashboard.
  • 8. Scope and commercial state drifta new idea enters as a ticket with a price. Build starts when the checklist is green. Support has a boundary and a date.
  • 9. Security and compliance not reusableone control pack and one approved reference setup, EU by default, written once and sent with every proposal.
  • 10. Single-threaded knowledgeevery client's rules in a file with their name on it, every system's memory in a database, every incident with a second owner.
  • 11. Reporting that advocatesevery finding carries its query and its number, graded proven, likely, inconclusive or disproven. Red is allowed to exist.
  • 12. Reuse still aspirationalthe small box, the access product, the eval kit, the health board and the control pack are versioned products every pod starts from.
Part 7

What I would build first

The first twelve weeks, and the loop after that.

The factory, in order
  • Weeks 1-4: the health boardour system, the people and the client system on one list, in Slack, with a silence alarm. Start with Bizquip and CWSI, because they are live.
  • Weeks 1-4: six states of doneevery project re-scored against the six states, with thresholds written down. Red allowed.
  • Weeks 5-8: the small box and the access productone connector kit for on-premise systems, one consent link for mailboxes, shadow mode for both. Reused on Johnston and every client after.
  • Weeks 5-8: the eval kitanswer keys from the client's own past decisions, replay before release, and prompt rules measured on real output. Tecknuovo first.
  • Weeks 9-12: the control pack and the reference setupwritten once, EU by default, sent with every proposal. Cyber Essentials Plus started in week one.
  • Always: the loopevery project leaves one reusable improvement behind, and the factory is measured by how many pods start from it.
